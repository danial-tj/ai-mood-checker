# Research foundation for the proactive Mood Checker

Reviewed: 6 October 2026.

## Purpose and evidence standard

The product should help a person choose a manageable action, make room for it, and notice whether it was useful. Reporting a mood score is not the intended outcome. Mood and energy are optional, separate inputs; the person remains the authority on how they feel and what they want to do.

This is a focused evidence review for product design, not a systematic review, clinical protocol, or claim that this app improves mental health. It draws on peer-reviewed reviews, meta-analyses, and a large randomized trial. A researcher's PhD is not itself evidence that a claim is true. We consider study design, population, intervention, outcomes, uncertainty, and relevance to the actual feature.

Evidence for a multi-session treatment does not establish the effectiveness of a short app prompt inspired by one of its ideas. Use **research-informed support**, not **clinically proven**, **PhD-proven**, or **treatment**. The complete Mood Checker intervention has not been clinically evaluated.

## Research and its limits

### 1. Personally meaningful activities

**Uphoff E et al. (2020). Behavioural activation therapy for depression in adults. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews.**

- Design and population: systematic review of 53 randomized trials with 5,495 adults with depression or depressive symptoms. Most interventions lasted 4-16 weeks.
- Relevant finding: behavioural activation, which includes scheduling and reviewing meaningful activities, may improve depression compared with usual care.
- Limits: certainty was mostly low to moderate; most findings were short-term. These were broader therapeutic programmes, commonly involving professional support, not five-minute app prompts for a general student population.
- Product implication: offer a small activity the user finds worthwhile, enjoyable, or connecting. Academic productivity is one possible direction, not the definition of wellbeing.
- Source: [Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013305_behavioural-activation-therapy-depression-adults). DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD013305.pub2.

### 2. A specific cue for a chosen action

**Sheeran P, Listrom O, Gollwitzer PM (2024, online publication). The when and how of planning: Meta-analysis of the scope and components of implementation intentions in 642 tests. European Review of Social Psychology.**

- Design and population: meta-analysis of 642 experimental tests across populations and cognitive, emotional, and behavioural outcomes.
- Relevant finding: linking a recognizable situation with a planned response can help follow-through. Effects varied; contingent if-then plans and rehearsing a plan were useful features.
- Limits: searches ran through September 2019 and the review was not preregistered. Broad planning outcomes do not establish improved mood in working students. A supplied plan cannot replace wanting and being able to act.
- Product implication: offer an optional editable cue: “When I get home, I will spend five minutes on the first paragraph.” Let the person change or remove it.
- Source: [Published paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563). The authors' accepted manuscript was also inspected for methods and limitations.

### 3. Reviewing progress

**Harkin B et al. (2016). Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence. Psychological Bulletin, 142(2), 198-229.**

- Design and population: 138 studies with 19,951 participants randomly allocated to progress-monitoring interventions or control conditions across goal domains.
- Relevant finding: encouraging progress monitoring improved goal attainment on average.
- Limits: goal attainment is not wellbeing. This does not establish that frequent mood tracking improves mental health, or that public sharing is appropriate for this product.
- Product implication: keep completion and helpfulness separate. A completed activity may be unhelpful; a partial attempt may be useful. Records remain private unless the person deliberately shares them.
- Source: [Paper record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/). DOI: 10.1037/bul0000025.

### 4. Comfortable physical activity

**Clegg AJ et al. (2026). Exercise for depression. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews.**

- Design and population: updated systematic review of exercise in adults with depression. Its current plain-language summary reports 73 studies involving at least 4,985 adults.
- Relevant finding: exercise may reduce depressive symptoms compared with no therapy.
- Limits: small studies, risk of bias, and limited long-term follow-up reduce confidence. Full exercise interventions are not equivalent to a brief movement suggestion. Do not claim our prompts work as well as medication or psychological treatment.
- Product implication: offer user-selected movement with a type and duration that are comfortable and accessible. Do not prescribe intensity, require walking, or promise a change after a fixed number of minutes.
- Source: [Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD004366_exercise-effective-treating-depression). DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD004366.pub7.

### 5. Protecting sleep opportunities

**Scott AJ et al. (2021). Improving sleep quality leads to better mental health: A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. Sleep Medicine Reviews, 60, 101556.**

- Design and population: 65 trials, covering 72 interventions and 8,608 adults.
- Relevant finding: interventions that successfully improved sleep also improved several mental-health outcomes on average.
- Limits: inclusion required a statistically significant sleep improvement. This does not show that every sleep intervention or generic tip works, or directly validate a short rest break or this planner.
- Product implication: let users protect a chosen wind-down period and leave time unallocated. Rest is a legitimate choice; do not fill every free evening minute with tasks. This is a design adaptation, not insomnia treatment.
- Source: [Published paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2021.101556).

### 6. Relevant evidence in university students

**Freeman D et al. (2017). The effects of improving sleep on mental health (OASIS): a randomised controlled trial with mediation analysis. The Lancet Psychiatry, 4(10), 749-758.**

- Design and population: randomized trial of 3,755 students with insomnia at 26 UK universities, comparing structured digital cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia with usual care.
- Relevant finding: the intervention improved insomnia and some mental-health outcomes.
- Limits: participants were selected for insomnia. Primary outcomes included insomnia, paranoia, and hallucinatory experiences; this was not a trial of everyday mood suggestions. A bedtime reminder does not reproduce the programme.
- Product implication: take students' sleep seriously without generating clinical sleep techniques or implying that this app delivers the studied treatment.
- Source: [Paper record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28888927/). DOI: 10.1016/S2215-0366(17)30328-0.

## Mapping evidence to the experience

| Product step | Intended behaviour | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Optional check-in | Ask about feeling and energy separately; include skip and unsure. | Context, not a diagnostic test. These studies do not validate inferring emotion from a calendar or agent conversation. |
| Choose a direction | Offer progress, a meaningful activity, comfortable movement, or rest. | This menu is a product adaptation, not a validated treatment. |
| Offer a feasible action | Fit the chosen activity to time, explicit energy, and commitments. Allow editing or declining. | Feasibility checks are engineering safeguards, not proof of psychological benefit. |
| Optional cue | Let the person specify when or where they intend to act. | Broad planning evidence does not guarantee follow-through or mood improvement. |
| Review completion | Record done, partly done, or skipped. | Completion is not proof the person feels better. |
| Review helpfulness | Separately ask helped, did not help, or unsure; allow skip. | This describes an experience, not a causal finding or treatment effect. |
| Adapt suggestions | Respect corrections, dismissed activities, and stated preferences. | Sparse feedback must not become a confident “this fixes your mood” rule. |

Examples are choices: “Do five minutes of a hobby you miss,” “Choose a little movement that feels comfortable,” or “Leave this time free to rest.” Durations are adjustable practical starting points, not evidence-based doses. A brief rest break must not be advertised as a tested sleep intervention.

Use a visible “Research behind this idea” link where relevant. Suggested concise wording:

> These ideas draw on published research. Our short prompts have not been clinically tested, and different things help different people.

Agent integrations must preserve these boundaries. Share only context the person explicitly agreed to share, and distinguish their own report from an agent's interpretation. Do not diagnose mood from calendar load, completion, silence, or message sentiment. Let the person change or withdraw a check-in.

## A practical first pilot

This proposed formative usability pilot is not a clinical trial. **No participants have been contacted or recruited.**

### Participants and duration

- Five adults, aged 18 or older, who combine study and paid work.
- Two weeks of voluntary use in their own routines.
- Before participation, explain the purpose, collected data, who can see it, and how to stop or delete it. Study or employment obligations must not depend on participation.
- People choose their own activities and may skip check-ins, actions, feedback, or the pilot. Do not promise treatment of a condition.

### Procedure

1. At the start, demonstrate a check-in, one chosen action, changing or declining it, and separate completion/helpfulness feedback. Ask what would be useful or intrusive.
2. During the two weeks, offer at most one optional daily invitation under the participant's notification preferences. Extra use is participant-initiated. Do not require an action every day.
3. After an attempted action, optionally ask whether it happened, was useful, or created difficulty. Missing feedback remains missing, not “not helpful.”
4. At each week's end, ask whether suggestions fit real schedules, whether questions or reminders were burdensome, and which suggestions to keep or remove.
5. At the end, review feedback with the participant, ask whether they would choose to keep using the app, and offer data deletion.

### Measures

| Measure | Question or observation |
| --- | --- |
| Usefulness | “Did this help with something that mattered to you?” Include yes, a little, no, unsure, and skip. |
| Feasibility | “Did this fit the time and energy you actually had?” Record conflicts and necessary resizing. |
| Burden | “Were the check-ins or reminders too much?” Note pauses and muting without treating them as failure. |
| Follow-through | Separate accepting, attempting, partial completion, completion, and skipping where available. Accepting is not doing. |
| Unwanted effects | Ask about pressure, guilt, discomfort, lost sleep, or other problems. Do not require sensitive explanations. |
| User control | Check correcting context, choosing rest, declining actions, changing reminders, and deleting data. |

Collect only the minimum voluntary data needed. This pilot does not require private agent transcripts, contact lists, raw calendar titles, diagnoses, or medical histories. Use participant codes in shared findings. Agree a limited retention period before collection and honour earlier deletion requests.

### Interpretation

Report counts with denominators and concrete examples, including unhelpful suggestions and missing responses. Five participants and no control group cannot establish that the app caused improvements. Self-reported helpfulness can guide product changes, but cannot justify clinical claims or general benefit to all working students.

Use the pilot to refine, remove, or retain features. Repeated infeasible suggestions, increased pressure, sleep disruption, confusing controls, or privacy problems warrant changes before expansion. Completion rates must never override reported harm or burden.

## Claims to avoid

- “Your mood is low because your calendar is busy.”
- “This activity will improve your mood in five minutes.”
- “Completing more tasks means you are healthier.”
- “Our app provides behavioural activation therapy or CBT for insomnia.”
- “A small uncontrolled pilot proves this improves people's lives.”

The intended promise is narrower and testable: **help people choose something useful that fits their day, and learn from what they say actually helped.**
